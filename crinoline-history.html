A short history of the crinoline

How a stiff horsehair fabric became a steel cage, and how the dream of the full skirt keeps returning.

The name

Horsehair and linen

The word crinoline comes from the French crin, meaning horsehair, and lin, meaning linen. In the 1830s and 1840s, petticoats made of this stiff fabric helped skirts stand away from the body.

To get the fashionable dome shape, women often wore several heavy petticoats at once, which was hot and cumbersome.

Timeline

Key moments

  1. 1830s–1840s

    Horsehair crinoline petticoats and multiple underskirts create rounded, fuller skirts.

  2. Mid-1850s

    The cage crinoline arrives: a frame of flexible steel hoops joined by tapes. It is lighter and lets skirts grow much wider.

  3. Early 1860s

    Skirts reach their widest. The shape gradually moves back, flattening at the front.

  4. 1870s

    The crinolette and then the bustle take over, concentrating volume at the back.

  5. Late 1940s–1950s

    Post-war fashion brings back tiny waists and full skirts, supported by layered net petticoats.

  6. Today

    Hoops and petticoats live on in bridal wear, formal gowns, dance costumes and historical reenactment.

Daily life

Living in a wide skirt

Cage crinolines were praised for their lightness and freedom of movement compared with stacks of petticoats. But very wide skirts could be awkward in doorways and carriages, and they were a real danger near open fires.

Cartoonists of the day loved to poke fun at the shape, yet women of many backgrounds wore some form of it at the height of the fashion.
